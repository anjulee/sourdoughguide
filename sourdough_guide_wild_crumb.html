Your complete guide to wild ferment

From ancient Egyptian bakeries to your kitchen — history, culture, and everything you need to bake your first loaf.

A brief history

Sourdough is the oldest leavened bread in human history. Until commercial yeast was invented less than 200 years ago, it was the only way bread could rise. Every culture that baked bread, baked sourdough.

~4,000–3,000 BCE · Ancient Egypt

The accidental discovery

Earliest evidence of leavened bread comes from Egypt — likely discovered when wild yeast drifted into wet grain. Egyptians had over 170 words for bread. Bakeries found at Giza fed the builders of the pyramids. Bakers kept cultures alive by saving raw dough from each batch — exactly as we do today.

~800 BCE · Greece & Rome

Symbol of civilization

Greeks considered leavened bread a mark of civilized life and passed the knowledge to Rome, where the government distributed hundreds of thousands of free loaves every month. Roman legions spread sourdough traditions across Europe.

Medieval · Monasteries

Kept alive in monasteries

After Rome's fall, monasteries became centres of bread and beer production. Monks in France, Germany, and Britain quietly maintained starter cultures for centuries, preserving the tradition through political upheaval.

Medieval–early modern · Eastern Europe

Rye, kvass, and the Slavic world

Across Poland, Russia, Ukraine, and the Baltic states, sourdough became inseparable from daily survival. Rye bread requires sourdough to be digestible. Starters (zakwas, zakvaská) were passed down in families for generations. Stale rye was also fermented into kvass, consumed across the Slavic world for over a thousand years.

1848 · California Gold Rush

The sourdoughs of San Francisco

French bakers brought levain to San Francisco, where the cool, foggy climate produced a uniquely tangy bread. Miners were nicknamed "sourdoughs." The Boudin Bakery has kept its original starter alive since 1849.

19th–20th century

Commercial yeast almost wins

Commercial baker's yeast gradually replaced sourdough across much of the world. Germany, Scandinavia, and Eastern Europe were exceptions — rye bread requires sourdough, so the tradition never died out.

1980s–present

The global revival

An artisan bread movement revived sourdough from the 1980s onwards. The 2020 pandemic brought a massive new wave of home bakers. Today it has returned to kitchens worldwide — as bread, but also as a connection to something ancient, patient, and alive.

Sourdough around the world

Wild fermentation evolved independently across the globe, adapting to local grains, climates, and traditions.

France

Levain & pain de campagne

French bakers refined wild-yeast bread into the boule and rustic country loaf. French baking law still regulates what can be sold as genuine sourdough.

Germany & Austria

Sauerteig & dark rye

Rye flour contains very little gluten — baker's yeast alone cannot raise it. Sourdough's acidity deactivates enzymes that would otherwise collapse the bread. Pumpernickel and Vollkornbrot have been made this way for centuries.

Italy

Lievito madre & panettone

Italian bakers maintained lievito madre (mother yeast) — a stiff starter kept almost like a dough. The foundation of panettone, one of the most technically demanding feats in baking.

Belgium & Netherlands

Desem

Flemish bakers developed desem (the word simply means "starter"), a stiff whole-wheat sourdough culture traditionally buried in flour to prevent drying out.

Eastern Europe — a sourdough heartland

No region has a more intimate daily relationship with sourdough. Here it was necessity, not a hobby — starters passed down in families for generations.

Poland

Zakwas & zurek — a sour rye soup fermented for days, still a beloved national dish.

Russia & Ukraine

Borodinsky dark rye + kvass, brewed from stale sourdough bread for over 1,000 years.

Baltics & Balkans

Latvian rupjmaize (legally protected), Romanian maia, starters kept in wooden vessels for decades.

Czech & Slovakia

Kvásek & caraway rye — kvásek shares its root with kvass. Fermentation runs deep through the language.

Ethiopia & Eritrea

Injera

A large, spongy flatbread made from teff. The batter ferments for two to three days, attracting wild yeasts — no separate starter is used. The cooked injera serves as both plate and utensil.

Sudan

Kisra

A fermented flatbread made from sorghum flour. The batter sours overnight before being cooked into a thin, crepe-like bread — a daily staple eaten with stews and lentils.

Egypt (ancient)

The cradle of leavened bread

Ancient Egyptians are credited with discovering leavened bread. Hieroglyphics depict bakery scenes, and ancient bakery remains have been uncovered at Giza.

San Francisco, USA

The iconic tang

San Francisco's cool, foggy climate produced a uniquely sour bread. The Boudin Bakery has kept its original starter alive since 1849. The specific bacteria was named Fructilactobacillus sanfranciscensis after the city.

Alaska & the Yukon

Survival bread

During the Klondike Gold Rush (1890s), prospectors carried sourdough starters as survival tools in brutal winters — keeping them warm against their bodies.

Mexico

Birote salado

In Guadalajara, a crusty sourdough roll evolved from French baguette traditions. The local altitude and microbes give it a flavour said to be impossible to replicate anywhere else.

India

Idli & dosa

Rice and lentil batter fermented overnight. Steamed into soft idli cakes or spread thin as crispy dosas — one of India's most beloved breakfast traditions.

Middle East

Flatbreads & ancient tradition

The Fertile Crescent — where agriculture began — is where early fermented flatbreads were almost certainly first made. Middle Eastern bakers still use naturally fermented doughs in flatbreads like pita and markook.

Central Asia

Tandyr breads

Across Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, and Tajikistan, naturally fermented doughs were baked in tandyr ovens. The resulting disc-shaped breads (non or lepyoshka) have a chewy, complex flavour from long, slow fermentation.

Your starter

A starter is flour and water that captures wild yeast and bacteria from the air and the flour itself. Fed regularly, it stays alive indefinitely — some have been passed down for generations.

The golden rule: your starter must be active, bubbly, and at or near its peak height before you use it. A weak starter = flat, dense bread.

Creating from scratch (days 1–14)

Day 1

Mix equal parts flour and water

50g wholemeal or rye flour + 50g room-temperature water. Stir, cover loosely (not airtight), leave at room temperature.

Days 2–5

Discard and feed daily

Pour away roughly half, then add 50g fresh flour + 50g water. Bubbles may appear. An odd smell at first is normal.

Days 6–10

Activity increases

Starter should reliably rise and fall after feedings. Mark the jar with a rubber band to track growth. A good starter doubles in 4–8 hours.

Days 10–14

Ready to bake with

When it consistently doubles, smells pleasantly sour (like yoghurt or beer), and a spoonful dropped in water floats, it's ready.

Ongoing

Store in the fridge

Once established, keep in the fridge between bakes. Feed once a week. Take it out the night before baking, feed it, and let it peak at room temperature.

Feeding ratios

A feeding ratio = starter : flour : water. Always feed by weight. The most common ratio for beginners is 1:1:1.

Your first loaf

Four ingredients: flour, water, salt, and starter. Watch the dough, not the clock.

1

Feed your starter

Feed so it peaks when you're ready to mix. Plan 4–8 hours ahead.

2

Mix the dough (autolyse)

400g white wheat + 100g whole wheat flour + 325g water. Mix until no dry flour remains. Cover and rest 30 minutes. This helps gluten develop without kneading.

3

Add starter and salt

Add 100g active (peaked) starter and 9g salt. Squeeze and fold until fully incorporated. Sticky is normal.

4

Stretch and fold

Every 30 minutes: grab one side, stretch up, fold over. Rotate, repeat 4 times. Do 4 sets over 2 hours. Dough becomes smoother and more elastic with each set.

After the last set the dough should feel noticeably more cohesive and less sticky.

5

Bulk fermentation (3–6 hours)

Leave to rise at room temperature until the dough has grown 50–75% and looks airy. Temperature affects this greatly — see weather tips.

6

Shape

Turn onto a lightly floured surface. Fold into a round or oval, building surface tension. Place seam-side up in a floured banneton or bowl lined with a floured cloth.

7

Cold proof overnight

Cover and refrigerate 8–16 hours. Slows fermentation, builds flavour, and makes scoring easier.

8

Bake in a Dutch oven

Preheat oven to 230–250°C with Dutch oven inside (45 min preheat). Score the top, bake 20 min covered then 20–25 min uncovered until deep golden brown.

Why a Dutch oven? It traps steam so the crust stays flexible and the loaf rises fully. Remove the lid to get a crispy, crackly crust.

9

Cool completely before slicing

Wait at least 1–2 hours. The crumb is still setting. Slicing too early makes it gummy.

Weather tips

The sweet spot is 24–26°C. Your starter, dough, and air all interact. Click a condition.

Ideal conditions — just bake normally

Standard 1:1:1 feeding ratio. Starter peaks in 4–8 hours.

Bulk fermentation takes 4–6 hours. Look for 50–75% dough growth.

Room temperature water works perfectly. No adjustments needed.

Hot weather — slow it down

Use cold or iced water when mixing dough and feeding starter — most effective way to slow fermentation.

Reduce starter amount: 15–20g at 25°C; as little as 5–10g overnight above 25°C.

Reduce hydration slightly if dough feels sticky — drop water by 5–10g.

Watch the dough, not the clock — bulk fermentation can finish in 2–3 hours.

Use cool surfaces — marble or stone. Chill bowls in the fridge before use.

Cold weather — keep it warm

Use warm water (up to 40°C) when mixing. Aim for 24–26°C dough temperature after mixing.

Find a warm spot — near the oven, on top of the fridge, or oven with just the light on.

Expect everything to take longer — bulk fermentation may take 8–12 hours.

Use slightly more starter to compensate for slow fermentation.

Boost with rye flour — ferments faster, helps sluggish starters in the cold.

Humid weather — reduce moisture

Reduce water by 10–20g — humidity adds moisture through the air.

Use less starter — humidity accelerates fermentation on top of temperature.

If humidity exceeds 50% — reduce hydration. Dough feels much wetter than the recipe suggests.

Watch your banneton — high humidity encourages mould. Dry thoroughly and dust generously.

Slice sooner — leaving bread on the counter for hours in humidity softens the crust.

Troubleshooting

My bread is flat and dense

• Starter wasn't active enough — use it only at peak, when bubbly and doubled.

• Under-fermented dough — the dough needs to grow 50–75% before shaping.

• Not enough stretch-and-fold sets — gluten needs to be developed.

My crumb is gummy or doughy inside

• Sliced too early — wait at least 1–2 hours after baking.

• Under-baked — internal temperature should reach 96–98°C.

• Weak starter — the dough didn't fully develop.

The dough spread sideways instead of rising

• Over-proofed — reduce room-temperature fermentation time by 1–2 hours.

• Poor shaping — not enough surface tension created.

• Too high hydration — try reducing water by 10–20g.

My bread is way too sour

• Starter too acidic — feed more frequently before baking to mellow it.

• Over-fermented dough — shorten the bulk ferment.

• Long cold proof — reduce overnight proof to 8–10 hours.

My crust is pale and soft, not crispy

• Under-fermented — sugars haven't developed enough for caramelisation.

• Lid left on too long — remove Dutch oven lid after 20 minutes.

• Oven not hot enough — preheat fully for at least 45 minutes at 230–250°C.

My starter won't activate

• Too cold — move to above 22°C (oven with just the light on).

• Chlorinated water — use filtered water or leave tap water in an open jug overnight.

• Needs more time — a new starter can take 10–14 days. Feed twice a day.

• Add a pinch of rye flour — packed with wild yeast, it kick-starts activity.

What you need

Highlighted cards are non-negotiable. The rest are helpful but can be improvised.

Kitchen scale

Non-negotiable. Sourdough is measured in grams, not cups.

Dutch oven

Essential for trapping steam and getting a proper crust and rise.

Glass jar

For your starter. Transparent so you can see activity. Loosely covered.

Thermometer

Check water, dough, and room temperature. Very helpful.

Banneton

For shaping. A floured bowl lined with a cloth towel works just as well.

Lame or sharp blade

For scoring. A very sharp serrated knife also works.

Flour tip: Start with white wheat (400g) + whole wheat (100g). As you get comfortable, explore rye, spelt, or stronger bread flour for new flavours and textures.
